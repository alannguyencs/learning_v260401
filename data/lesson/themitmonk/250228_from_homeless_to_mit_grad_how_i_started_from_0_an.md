## I. Find the Right Product

- Skip the grand mission — solve a real problem first. Microsoft started as two guys writing software; Facebook started as FaceMash. Theranos and FTX had inspiring missions and the founders are in prison.
- Chase problems, not passion — Airbnb was born from two broke founders who couldn't afford a hotel. Sandeep's search engine company got crushed by Google, so they pivoted: sold search to Yahoo, rebuilt as B2B information retrieval, and Microsoft bought them for $1.2B. Passions don't last, but problems do.
- Subtract ruthlessly — Southwest built a giant business by removing fancy meals, reserved seats, and big airport reps. Steve Jobs saved Apple by cutting almost all product lines to focus on iMac. The most important subtraction is internal: focus on the essential, subtract the noise — that's what preserves your sanity.
- Find founder-market fit — Melanie Perkins was a design instructor who saw people struggle daily. That pain became Canva.
- Find product-market fit — Burbn pivoted to photo sharing and became Instagram. Track activation rate, retention rate, and churn rate.

## II. Understand Your Customers

- Run experiments instead of trusting your ego — Sandeep's company discovered a winning pricing model (volume-based) they'd never considered through A/B testing.
- Sell promises, not products: Nike beat Adidas not with better shoes but by selling a dream.
- Watch what customers do, not what they say — Sandeep's AI fraud company heard endless praise from prospects who never bought, then retargeted high-urgency customers for explosive growth.
- Test willingness to pay early: startups that charged found 90% retention dropped to 10%, revealing they never had real product-market fit.

## III. Lead the Organization

- Hire people who adapt — every company Sandeep joined changed direction within 18 months. Pay them top dollar, give equity, make them owners.
- Build processes: daily standups, weekly one-on-ones, Monday exec meetings, company all-hands — over-communicate so anyone can decide without you.
- Use OKRs (read "Measure What Matters" by John Doerr) — 3-4 per quarter, cascaded down to function, team, individual.
- Guard cash above all — a CEO resigned after SVB swept $10M overnight due to untracked loan covenants. Revenue, margin, profit are accounting; only cash is real. Cash is the vital oxygen for your business.
- Lead with love: people first, product second, profits third.

## Story

Linh runs a bubble tea shop in Ho Chi Minh City. She started with a grand vision — "Revolutionize Vietnamese beverages" — printed on banners, business cards, even the napkins. Nobody cared. She was losing money every week.

One afternoon she noticed office workers rushing past her shop at 2pm, exhausted and thirsty, buying canned coffee from a convenience store next door. That was a real problem: tired people who needed a quick afternoon pick-me-up. She scrapped the grand mission and made a simple menu — three teas, fast service, out the door in 90 seconds. She cut the fancy toppings, the Instagram-worthy layered drinks, the ten flavors nobody ordered. Three drinks. That's it. Just like Southwest Airlines cutting meals and reserved seats — subtraction made her faster and cheaper than everyone else.

Her friend Tuan, a former barista, joined as co-founder. He'd spent five years making drinks for office workers. He knew exactly what they wanted before they said it — strong tea, less sugar, easy to carry. That was founder-market fit. Customers trusted him because he'd walked in their shoes.

Then Linh made a mistake. She asked customers what new drinks they wanted. They said mango smoothies, fruit teas, matcha lattes — the works. She added six new items. Sales dropped. The kitchen slowed down, the 90-second promise broke, and regulars stopped coming. So she stopped listening to what customers said and watched what they actually ordered. Three drinks still made up 85% of sales. She cut the rest. Growth came back.

Her competitor across the street offered free samples every day. Crowds lined up, took the free tea, and walked away. When he started charging, almost nobody came back. Free was a hobby, not a business. Linh tested her prices early — she raised the price by 5,000 dong and nobody left. That told her the product was delivering real value.

As the shop grew to three locations, Linh realized she couldn't be everywhere. She hired Minh, a quiet guy who'd worked at four different food stalls in three years — not because he was flaky, but because each stall had changed its menu and he'd adapted every time. That was the kind of person she needed. She gave him a share of the profits and a Monday morning meeting where all three shop managers talked through the week. She set one goal per quarter — just one — and made sure everyone knew it.

One month, Linh got excited about revenue. The shops brought in 200 million dong. But when she checked the bank account, only 40 million sat there — suppliers, rent, and a delayed payment from a catering client had eaten the rest. She remembered: revenue is not cash. She started tracking cash weekly, like oxygen.

And through it all, she took care of her people first. She paid Minh above market rate. She bought the team lunch on Saturdays. When Minh's mother got sick, she covered his shifts without docking pay. Minh, in turn, treated every customer like a friend. And those customers never went to the competitor across the street.

**The lesson:** Skip the mission, solve the pain. Subtract until you're fast. Watch what people do, not what they say. And take care of your people — they'll take care of everything else.
