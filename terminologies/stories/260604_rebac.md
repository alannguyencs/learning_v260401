# ReBAC

Let me start with the question that sits underneath everything we're going to talk about today, because if you keep this one question in your head the rest will fall into place on its own. Every time a user touches your system, you have to answer: **can this person do this thing to this resource — yes or no?** That's it. That's authorization. And I want you to notice straight away that this is a *different* question from "who are you?" — proving who you are is authentication, the login step, and by the time we get to authorization that part is already done. The user is standing inside the building. Now we're deciding which rooms they're allowed to walk into. So whenever you see an access-control rule, train yourself to read it as a little sentence: *this subject, doing this action, on this resource — allow or deny?* Everything else is just different ways of answering that one sentence.

```
subject ──action──> resource  ?  →  allow / deny
```


Now, the oldest and simplest way to answer it is to look only at the user and ask, "what is this person?" We hand people labels — we call them **roles** — like `admin`, `editor`, `viewer`, and we bundle a pile of permissions into each label. If you hold the `editor` role, you can edit. This is **RBAC, role-based access control**, and it's everywhere because it's wonderfully easy to reason about. But I want you to feel exactly where it runs out of room. Say Alan has the `editor` role. That means Alan can edit *any* post on the whole site, because the rule never once looks at *which* post we're talking about — it looks only at Alan's label. The moment you want to say "Alan can edit *his own* posts but not Chloe's," RBAC has nothing to grab onto, because that distinction doesn't live in Alan's role at all. It lives in the *relationship* between Alan and a *particular* post.

```
RBAC:   user ──has──> role  →  allow   (the post is never looked at)
```


So people reached for the next idea, which is: don't look only at the user — look at **the thing being touched, too**. Compare details of the user against details of the specific resource. This is **ABAC, attribute-based access control**, and it solves exactly the case RBAC couldn't. Now I can write "you may edit a post only if you are its author," and the system compares the post's author field against the person asking. Alan edits his own posts, Chloe's stay locked to him. Lovely. But — and this is the hinge of the whole lesson, so slow down here — ABAC can only compare the **direct details** of the two things sitting right in front of it. It can ask "is this post's author *you*?" because the author is written right there on the post. What it *cannot* gracefully ask is anything that has to travel **through other things** to be true. "Can you edit this task because you're on a team that owns the project this task belongs to?" Notice how that sentence hops: *you* → your *team* → a *project* → the *task*. ABAC has no good way to walk that chain. The truth isn't in the direct attributes of you and the task; it's strung out across a path of relationships in between.

```
ABAC:  user.attr  ──compare──>  resource.attr   →  allow   (direct details only)
hop:   you → team → project → task              (ABAC can't walk this)
```


And once you say the word **path**, you've basically invented the next idea on your own. Picture your whole organisation drawn as a giant map. The **dots** on the map are things — people, teams, documents, tasks. The **lines** between the dots are relationships — *manages*, *is a member of*, *owns*, *contains*. Your company org chart, plus every "who owns what" link, *is* this map. And here's the beautiful part: each individual line is just one dead-simple fact you wrote down once, like "Bob can edit document X" or "Alan manages Chloe." Nothing clever in any single line. The power shows up when you start **following the lines**. To check whether someone's allowed to reach something, you put your finger on the person, and you trace: *Alan manages Chloe, Chloe is in ProjectAlpha, ProjectAlpha contains Task #42* — so Alan can reach Task #42. You didn't write a rule about Alan and Task #42. You just traced a route. This map is the **relationship graph**, and following its lines is how you answer that hop-through-other-things question that defeated ABAC.

```
Alan ──manages──> Chloe ──member_of──> ProjectAlpha ──contains──> Task#42
   └──────────────── follow the lines: Alan can reach Task#42 ──────────┘
```


So the graph is powerful — but I want you to be honest with yourself about what it *costs*, because this is where a lot of real systems make a quiet, smart decision. To use the graph for real, you have to **build the whole map and store it**, you have to keep every single line up to date as people change teams and hand off documents, and you have to walk it on every request. That usually means standing up a separate graph database and praying it never drifts out of sync with reality. And here's the thing: a lot of the time, you don't actually need the whole map. Most real checks only follow **one short link** — "did *you* write this?", "do *you* manage this person?" — not some epic five-hop chain. So instead of storing the map, you can replace each line with a small **yes/no question you ask on the spot**, right against the live data, the moment you need the answer. "Is this your own row?" "Is this person someone you manage?" "Is anyone even logged in?" We call each of these little questions a **predicate**. Nothing to store, nothing to keep in sync — you ask, the live database answers. And when a decision could be reached more than one way, you just string the predicates together with **or**, so that passing *any one* of them lets you through. To see a salary row, the rule might be "is it your own salary?" *or* "are you this person's manager?" — Alan passes for his own pay, and he passes for Chloe's because he manages her, but a stranger's salary fails both questions and stays hidden. Same spirit as tracing lines on the graph — just far, far lighter.

```
predicate:  is_own?  OR  manages?  OR  logged_in?   →  ask live data, no map stored
```


Now, a predicate decides *whether* you're allowed — but allowed to see *how much*? This is where it gets practical, because the answer lands at one of three different **zoom levels** of a database table. Picture a table as a spreadsheet. There's the **whole sheet**, there are its **columns** running down — name, email, salary — and there are its **rows** running across, one record each, one employee per line. At the coarsest level, you can hide the entire sheet, so the user doesn't even know it exists — a regular employee querying the system simply never sees the `salaries` table or the `audit_logs` table; to them those tables aren't there at all. One level finer, you let them see the sheet but you **mask a column** — Alan opens the `employees` table, reads everyone's name and department happily, but the `salary` column comes back as `***` for everyone except himself. And at the finest level, you let them see the sheet and all its columns but only **certain rows** — and this one is done with a SQL **`WHERE` clause**, which is just the part of a query that keeps only the rows meeting some condition. The system quietly staples an extra condition onto the user's query — say `WHERE team = 'Chloe's team'` — so even if Chloe asked for *every* task in the company, the database hands back only her team's. These three stack: one request might hide a few tables outright, mask a column or two on the tables that survive, and trim the rows down to just yours — which is why two people running the *exact same query* can get completely different results.

```
table  →  hide whole sheet
column →  mask a field (salary → ***)
row    →  WHERE keeps only your rows
```


And now we can put the name on the whole thing, because everything we've built leads here. **ReBAC — relationship-based access control** — is the model that says you're allowed to touch something *only if there's a chain of relationships connecting you to it*. Hear how it differs from its cousins: RBAC asked "**what role do you have?**", ABAC asked "**do your details match this thing's details?**", and ReBAC asks "**is there a path from you to this thing?**" — answered either by following lines on the relationship graph, or, in a lighter system, by chaining those predicates together.

```
RBAC   →  what role do you have?
ABAC   →  do your details match the resource's?
ReBAC  →  is there a path from you to the resource?
```
 The chain can be short or long. A **direct link** — "you wrote this post, so you can edit it" — means the moment Alan writes a post, the right to edit it simply *exists*; nobody granted it. A **one-hop link** — "you manage this person, so you can see their salary" — means Alan sees Chloe's pay today, and the day Chloe moves to a different manager, Alan loses that access *on his own*, with no one needing to remember to revoke it. A **multi-hop link** rides a longer chain, *you → team → project → task*. And that self-correcting quality is the entire payoff: in RBAC you'd have to invent a fresh role for each of these situations — "post-owner," "salary-of-my-reports," "task-of-my-projects" — and then spend your life assigning and revoking them by hand. ReBAC writes none of that down case by case. It **works the answer out from the relationships you already have**, so the instant a relationship changes, every permission that depended on it changes with it, automatically.

Let me make this concrete, because there's a system I want you to picture — a natural-language-to-SQL agent we'll call `/librarian`, which guards every database read with exactly these predicates. Imagine three people ask it the *same* question: *"How much did we spend on reimbursements?"* Binh is a regular employee. Chloe manages Binh and two others. Alan is whitelisted — full access. The agent first checks whether the reimbursement table is even visible: the gate is "is it your own record, *or* do you manage that person, *or* are you whitelisted?" — and since "your own record" is true for everyone, all three see the table. Then it checks the columns; here both the username and the amount columns are open to any logged-in user, so there's no masking to do and the agent shows all three the same schema. So the language model writes the *identical* base query for everyone: `SELECT SUM(amount) FROM reimbursement_workflow`. The divergence happens entirely at the **row** level. For Binh, the whitelist question fails, "do you manage this person" fails, but "is it your own?" passes — so the system staples on `WHERE employee_username = 'binh'`, and Binh sees only his own claims. For Chloe, "is it your own?" passes *and* "do you manage this person?" passes — and answering that second one costs a single lookup, asking the employees table who reports to Chloe, which comes back Binh, David, Emma — so her query becomes `WHERE employee_username = 'chloe' OR employee_username IN ('binh','david','emma')`: herself plus her direct reports. And Alan is whitelisted, which short-circuits everything — full access, the query runs unchanged, he sees the whole company. Three people, one question, one base query, three different answers — and the *only* thing that separated them was which relationships connect them to the rows.

```
same query: SELECT SUM(amount) FROM reimbursement_workflow
  Binh  (self)        →  WHERE user = 'binh'
  Chloe (self+manages)→  WHERE user = 'chloe' OR user IN (reports)
  Alan  (whitelist)   →  (unchanged, whole company)
```
 Nobody was handed a new role; that one-hop "who do you manage" check is literally a graph traversal written as a SQL subquery.

The last thing I'll leave you with is where you'll bump into this in the wild, and the confusions to sidestep. The idea was popularised by **Google Zanzibar**, the global authorization system behind Drive, YouTube, and Cloud sharing — and there are open-source implementations of it, **OpenFGA** and **SpiceDB**, where you write a schema and store little relationship facts like `object#relation@user`. Every time Google Drive tells you "you can edit this file because you're in the team that owns the folder," that's textbook ReBAC. Now, the confusions. Don't mix up **ReBAC and RBAC** — a role is *global* and lives on the user, a relationship is *specific* to one user-and-resource pair. Don't mix up **ReBAC and ABAC** — ABAC compares attributes against a policy, ReBAC follows edges between entities, and the two actually *compose* nicely, since you can hang an attribute condition off a relationship. Watch the difference between **ReBAC and an old-fashioned access control list** — an ACL is a flat, hand-maintained list of who's allowed on each resource, whereas ReBAC *derives* that list from relationships, so it stays correct on its own; pull someone off a team and all their inherited access vanishes at once. And finally, the one that trips people up most: **ReBAC is not the same thing as a graph database.** ReBAC is the *model*, the idea — "allowed if a path connects you." A graph database is just one *way* to implement it. Our `/librarian` does honest ReBAC with nothing but plain Postgres subqueries and no graph engine anywhere in sight. Hold onto that distinction, hold onto the one sentence we started with — *subject, action, resource: is there a path?* — and you understand ReBAC.
